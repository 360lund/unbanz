Ways to get a banned Instagram account back, free options first.

Start with the free official routes. If they are closed or did not work, an independent service is one more option. Meta makes the final decision on every route.

Short answer

The best first route is the free in-app review: log in, follow the on-screen steps and submit once. If the account was hacked, use instagram.com/hacked. If you subscribe to Meta Verified, contact its support. If those are closed or failed, an independent service like Unbanz can prepare and handle the appeal. Meta makes the final decision.

Last updated: 28 October 2026.

The routes, in the order to try them

  1. In-app review or appeal (free). Log in, read the full notice and use the Disagree, Appeal or Request review button once. Add a short, factual note if there is a text box. Guide: how to appeal a suspension.
  2. instagram.com/hacked (free). For accounts where the password or email was changed by someone else. Use a device you have logged in from before. Guide: hacked Instagram account recovery.
  3. Meta Verified support (paid subscription). If you already have an active Meta Verified subscription, Instagram’s Help Center says you can contact support from the app where it is available. Compare it with the other routes on Compare options.
  4. Identity checks (free). If Instagram asks for a code or a video selfie, complete it. Many reviews wait on this step. Guide: video selfie verification.
  5. An independent service such as Unbanz (paid). We read the notice, say whether an appeal looks realistic, prepare it and handle it through Meta’s official process. Price is agreed before work starts.

Do not repeat the same request

Sending the same appeal again and again does not help. Submit once, keep a screenshot of the confirmation and watch your email and the app for the answer. If the answer is no, read what it says and see what to do after a denied appeal.

What does not work

  • Paying a stranger who says they know someone at Meta. No one outside Meta can change a decision.
  • Sharing your password or login codes with anyone.
  • Buying a replacement account, or an account that someone says is “restored”.
  • Filing false or mass reports against other accounts.
