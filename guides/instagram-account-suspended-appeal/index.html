Instagram account suspended: how to appeal

Short answer: tap "Disagree" on the suspension notice, confirm your details, and submit before the deadline (usually 180 days). Then wait for Instagram’s reply.

What “We suspended your account” means

Instagram’s Help Center says that accounts which don’t follow its Community Guidelines or Terms of Use may be suspended. If yours is, you are told by email and when you open the account in the app or a browser, and Instagram also tells you whether you can appeal if you believe it made a mistake. The instruction is the same each time: after logging in, follow the on-screen instructions to submit your appeal.

While an account is suspended it isn’t visible to other people and you can’t use it. A suspension is best thought of as the stage before a permanent decision: the account is on hold, and the appeal is your chance to change the outcome.

Suspended vs disabled

Instagram uses both words, and its Help Center does not draw a hard line between them. In practice, a suspension notice usually appears when you open the app, is often accompanied by an email, and tells you whether you can appeal. A disabled account is one that is no longer visible to others. Either way, the screen in front of you is the best guide: read the whole message, including small print about deadlines.

If you can still log in but some features are blocked, that is a restriction rather than a suspension. Check Settings > Account Status to see which content or activity was flagged.

Pay attention to the deadline

Many suspension screens show a countdown, commonly a number of days before the account is permanently disabled if you do not act. Instagram does not publish one fixed window for every account, so go by the date on your own notice and appeal as early as possible. Do not wait until the last day: verification steps can take time, and if the deadline passes the appeal option may no longer appear.

For comparison, Facebook’s Help Center states that a suspended Facebook account has 180 days to disagree with the decision before it is permanently disabled. Instagram notices often show a similar countdown, but treat your own screen as the source of truth.

What the 180-day window really means

Many suspension screens show a countdown such as a number of days left to appeal, and 180 days is the figure people most often see. Instagram’s own help pages don’t print a single fixed number for every account, so the deadline that counts is the one on your screen. Write it down the day you first see it.

Meta’s Facebook Help Center describes how the same window works for suspended Facebook accounts, and it is a useful guide to the logic: you can appeal the suspension within 180 days; if you don’t appeal in time, or the appeal isn’t successful, the account is permanently disabled and you can’t request another review. The same page notes that the appeal period varies by region and that Meta may disable an account without suspending it first for severe or time-sensitive violations.

Two common misreadings: the countdown is not a sentence you serve before the account comes back by itself, and it isn’t a reason to wait. It is a deadline to appeal. Nothing in Meta’s help pages suggests an account is restored automatically when the countdown ends.

Step-by-step: appealing in the app

1. Update the app and log in

Use the latest version of the Instagram app, or instagram.com in a browser, and log in to the suspended account. The appeal option only appears once you’re logged in.

2. Read the whole notice and capture it

Screenshot every screen. Note the policy named (if any), the date of the suspension and the number of days left. These details shape what you write and are the first thing anyone helping you will ask for.

3. Tap the appeal option

The button is usually labelled something like Appeal or Disagree with decision. Follow each step to the end. Leaving halfway can mean nothing was actually submitted.

4. Verify that you’re a real person

You may be asked to confirm a phone number or email, or to take a video selfie turning your head in different directions. Instagram’s video selfie help page says you won’t have access to the account while the selfie is reviewed, and that if it isn’t accepted you may be able to resubmit it in the app. Do this somewhere well lit, without a hat or sunglasses, and make sure it really is the account owner on camera.

5. Explain what happened briefly, if you’re given a box

Keep it factual: who you are, what the account is used for, and why the policy named doesn’t fit what happened. If you think someone else accessed the account, say so plainly. Avoid angry language, copied templates and anything untrue.

6. Wait and watch your email

Instagram doesn’t publish a review time. Check the email address linked to the account, including spam, and the app itself. Only trust messages that genuinely come from Instagram or Meta.

Why Instagram suspends accounts

The notice may name a specific policy. Instagram’s account notices and help pages mention reasons such as pretending to be someone else, hateful conduct, threats or targeting individuals, graphic violence and glorifying self-harm, alongside spam-like or inauthentic behaviour covered by Meta’s Community Standards. Meta’s Transparency Center also says it removes accounts created to get around previous restrictions. Some suspensions are triggered by automated systems, and some follow a hack, where someone else used the account in ways that broke the rules.

What to write (and what not to)

Not every flow gives you space to write, but when it does, keep it short and specific:

  • State that you are the owner and how you use the account (personal, creator, small business).
  • If you believe the action was triggered by something specific – a post that was misread, a sudden burst of activity, an unauthorised login – say so plainly.
  • If you have already removed a post that might have broken the rules, mention it.
  • Avoid threats, legal jargon, all-caps or long emotional messages. Reviewers need facts, not pressure.

Never include your password or one-time codes in an appeal, and never submit content you do not have the right to share.

Mistakes to avoid during a suspension

  • Waiting for the countdown to run out. Appeal early, while you can still gather information and complete verification without a rush.
  • Making a new account to carry on. Meta says it disables accounts created to get around restrictions, and it can drag the new account into the same problem.
  • Submitting half an appeal. Make sure you see a confirmation that your appeal was sent.
  • Trusting “appeal links” in DMs or emails from strangers. Fake appeal pages are a common way to steal passwords. Start from the app or from help.instagram.com.
  • Handing over your login. No genuine helper needs your password or two-factor codes.

After you submit

Instagram does not publish a review time. Check the email address on the account, including spam and promotions folders, and log in occasionally to see whether the status has changed. Be suspicious of direct messages or emails claiming to be from “Meta Support” that ask for payment or login details – Instagram does not ask for those through DMs.

If the appeal is successful, review what may have triggered the suspension: third-party automation tools, purchased engagement, repeated identical messages, or reposted content you do not own are frequent causes. Pre-appeal checklist

Before you tap the appeal button, spend ten minutes getting ready. It is the cheapest way to avoid a rushed, incomplete submission:

  • Screenshot the full suspension notice, including the deadline and any policy named.
  • Confirm you can receive codes on the email and phone linked to the account. If not, update them in Accounts Center from another logged-in device if you still have one.
  • Find a quiet, well-lit spot for a possible video selfie, with nothing covering your face.
  • Write down, in two or three sentences, how you use the account and why you think the decision is wrong.
  • Make a note of any recent unusual activity: new apps connected, logins from new places, a sudden burst of follows or messages.
  • Stop any automation or scheduling tool that logs in with your password until it is resolved.

If the account belongs to a business or a client, the person who normally logs in should complete the appeal from their usual device. Verification steps are built around the account owner, and switching people or devices halfway through can create mismatches.

If the appeal is rejected

Once an appeal fails, Meta’s help pages describe the decision as permanent, and the in-app option usually disappears. If you’re in the EU, you may also hear about independent out-of-court dispute bodies set up under the Digital Services Act; check the notice and the official Help Center for what applies to you. If the account was taken over before it was suspended, the hacked-account route at instagram.com/hacked may still be relevant. YOUR QUESTIONS

Frequently asked questions

How many times can I appeal a suspension?+

Usually once per decision. Make that first appeal complete and accurate.

Do I need to send ID to appeal?+

Sometimes. Instagram may ask for a video selfie. Only verify inside the official app or website.

What if I miss the appeal deadline?+

The account may be permanently disabled and the appeal option removed.

Does my Instagram account come back automatically after 180 days?+

Nothing in Meta’s help pages says so. The number shown is a deadline to appeal. Meta’s Facebook Help Center says that if you don’t appeal within the window, or the appeal fails, the account is permanently disabled.

How long does an Instagram suspension appeal take?+

Instagram doesn’t publish a fixed timeframe. Keep an eye on the email linked to the account and on the app after you submit.

Why does Instagram ask for a video selfie?+

To check that a real person owns the account. Instagram says you turn your head in different directions, you won’t have access while it’s reviewed, and you may be able to resubmit if it isn’t accepted.

Is it safe to use an Instagram appeal link someone sent me?+

Be very careful. Start from the Instagram app or help.instagram.com instead, and never enter your password or codes on a page someone sent you in a DM.
