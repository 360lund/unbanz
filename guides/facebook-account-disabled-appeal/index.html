Facebook account disabled or suspended: how to appeal

Facebook gives most suspended accounts a window to disagree before the decision becomes permanent. Here is how that process works and how to use it well.

Suspended vs permanently disabled

Facebook uses two different words, and they mean different things. Facebook’s Help Center page My personal Facebook account is suspended or disabled explains that you’ll be told by email, and when you log in you’ll see either We suspended your account or We disabled your account.

  • Suspended means Meta found that the account, or activity on it, may not follow the Community Standards. You can appeal the suspension within 180 days. While it is suspended, nobody can see the account and you can’t use it.
  • Disabled is the permanent stage. Facebook says that if you don’t appeal within 180 days, or your appeal isn’t successful, the account is permanently disabled and you won’t be able to request another review.

Two details on that page matter. First, the time you have to appeal varies by region, so trust the deadline on your own screen. Second, Meta may disable an account without suspending it first, for severe or time-sensitive violations.

Why Facebook suspends accounts

Facebook gives examples in its help page, including:

  • posting content that doesn’t follow the Community Standards;
  • misrepresenting your identity to mislead people or evade enforcement, including impersonating someone;
  • contacting people to harass them, or to advertise or promote in ways that aren’t allowed.

In some regions, accounts may also be suspended or disabled for people in a specific age range under local law. Meta’s Transparency Center adds that it works to disable accounts created to get around earlier restrictions, and that repeated strikes can lead to removal. Not every case is deserved: hacked accounts are sometimes disabled for activity the owner didn’t do, and automated systems do make mistakes.

How to disagree with the decision

1. Make sure it really is a suspension

If you don’t see a suspension or disabled notice, you may have a different problem. A changed password or email you didn’t make points to a hack, and Facebook says to visit facebook.com/hacked on a device you’ve used to log in before. “Your account is locked” screens follow a different unlock flow.

2. Capture the notice

Screenshot the full notice, the date and the number of days left to appeal. Save the email Facebook sent you. If you can still reach Account Status (click your profile picture, then Help & support › Account Status), note any strikes or restrictions listed for your profile and the Pages and groups you manage.

3. Log in and start the appeal

Facebook’s instruction is simple: log in to Facebook and you’ll be prompted to appeal the suspension. Follow every step until you see confirmation that the appeal was submitted.

4. Complete identity checks

You may be asked to confirm a phone number, solve a security check, take a video selfie or upload ID. Use your real details and do it carefully. Submitting edited or someone else’s documents will hurt your case.

5. Keep any explanation short and factual

If there’s space to explain, say who you are, how you use the account, and why you believe the decision is a mistake. If you think you were hacked, say so and give the approximate date. Don’t copy long templates or make claims you can’t back up.

6. Wait, and don’t resubmit

Facebook doesn’t publish a fixed review time. Check your email, including spam, and only trust messages that genuinely come from Facebook or Meta.

If you think the account was hacked

If the account was suspended after someone else got in – for example, scam posts you did not make – say so in the appeal and use Facebook’s official recovery tool at facebook.com/hacked. Secure the email address linked to the account first so the attacker cannot reset it again.

Tips for a stronger appeal

  • Act early in the 180-day window; do not wait until the end.
  • Make sure the name on the profile matches your ID if you are asked to verify.
  • Use the same device and location you normally log in from where possible.
  • Do not open a new personal account to get around the suspension – Meta may disable it as well.
  • Keep screenshots of the notice, dates and any reference numbers.

Pages, groups and business assets

A disabled profile affects more than your timeline. Facebook’s Business Help Center explains that if a personal profile is restricted from advertising, that person can’t create new ads, and ad accounts where they’re the only user may also be disabled. Other people with access to the same business portfolio, ad account or Page may still be able to advertise.

Pages also need someone in charge. Facebook says a Page that no longer has at least one person with full control is automatically deactivated and scheduled for deletion. If you run a business Page, make sure a trusted second person has full control. If your Page itself has a restriction, read our guide to a restricted or unpublished Facebook Page, and for advertising problems see Meta ad account restricted.

Mistakes that make it worse

  • Letting the window run out. After the appeal period, Facebook says you can’t request another review.
  • Creating a second profile to carry on. Meta disables accounts created to get around restrictions, and multiple or fake-name accounts are against the rules.
  • Using appeal “email addresses” from forums. Old lists of Facebook emails are not an appeal route. Use the notice and the current Help Center.
  • Sharing your password or codes. People who promise to fix your account in exchange for your login are a common source of hacks.
  • Paying anyone who promises results before reviewing your case. A real service checks the account first, then quotes.

Before you submit

Because you usually get one chance to disagree, preparation pays off:

  • Screenshot the notice, including the date and any policy mentioned.
  • Check you can receive codes on the email or phone linked to the account.
  • Have a government-issued ID ready in case you are asked, and confirm the name matches the profile.
  • Download a copy of your information if the notice offers it – it is useful even if the appeal fails.
  • Write two or three factual sentences about how you use Facebook and why you believe the decision is wrong.

Afterwards, check the email on the account regularly, including spam. Ignore messages from “Meta support” pages or profiles that contact you on Messenger asking for passwords, codes or payment – Facebook does not run appeals that way.

If the account is linked to a business, think ahead: an important Page or Business Portfolio should never depend on a single personal profile. Add at least one other trusted admin so the business keeps running if one profile is restricted.

YOUR QUESTIONS

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to appeal on Facebook?+

You have 180 days to disagree with the decision. After that, the account is permanently disabled.

Can I get my Facebook data while suspended?+

Often, yes. Check the notice for an option to download your information.

Should I create a new Facebook account?+

No. Meta may disable accounts made to get around restrictions, which makes things worse.

What happens if I miss the 180-day appeal window?+

Facebook says the account is then permanently disabled and you can’t request another review of the decision.

Can I appeal again after my Facebook appeal is rejected?+

Facebook’s help page says that if your appeal isn’t successful, the account is permanently disabled and you won’t be able to request another review.

Will my Facebook Page be deleted if my profile is disabled?+

Not automatically, but a Page with no remaining person who has full control is deactivated and scheduled for deletion. Make sure someone else you trust has full control.

Does Unbanz need my Facebook login?+

No. Send the profile link and the notice. Never share your password, login codes or recovery codes with anyone.

How long does a Facebook appeal take?+

Facebook doesn’t publish a fixed timeframe. Submit once, then watch your email and the Facebook app.
